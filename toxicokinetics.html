01 Foundations

Introduction & the Dose‑Response Relationship

The right dose differentiates a poison from a remedy. — Paracelsus, foundational principle of toxicology

Toxicology rests on one core principle: the relationship between the amount of a substance received (dose) and the harmful reaction it causes (response). This is not arbitrary — there is almost always a dose below which no measurable response occurs, known as the threshold doseThreshold dose: the minimum dose at which a toxic effect first appears — where the body's ability to detoxify or repair is exceeded.. Above that threshold, responses increase with dose until a maximum effect is reached.

🧬
Toxic Reactions vs. Allergic Reactions

A toxic effect results directly from a chemical acting on cells. An allergic response, by contrast, is the body releasing its own chemicals in response to a stimulus — not a direct toxic action. Allergies do not follow standard dose-response assumptions and represent a distinct immunological mechanism.

📊
Shape of the Dose-Response Curve

Plotting dose against response generates a characteristic S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve. Key features are the threshold (point where toxicity first appears), the slope (how rapidly the population responds per unit dose increase), and the plateau (maximum response). Different chemicals differ in where their threshold falls and how steep the slope is.

💡 Key Assumptions of Dose-Response

1. There is almost always a dose below which no response occurs. 2. A single chemical is responsible for the effect. 3. The response is related to the dose at the target site. These assumptions hold for most xenobioticsXenobiotic: any chemical foreign to the body, including drugs, pollutants, food additives, and environmental contaminants. but not for allergens or certain hormones.

⬇️
U-Shaped Curve

Seen with essential nutrients (vitamins, essential metals). Both deficiency and excess are harmful. The body needs a mid-range optimal zone. Examples: vitamins A, D; selenium; iron.

〰️
Hormesis

Some substances produce stimulatory (seemingly beneficial) effects at low doses and adverse effects at high doses. Observed with hormones, certain pesticides, and radiation. The mechanism often involves low-dose adaptive responses.

☢️
Cancer Dose-Response

For carcinogensCarcinogen: any substance capable of causing cancer in living tissue., risk is assumed to be linear with no safe threshold — any exposure theoretically increases cancer risk. The cancer slope factor quantifies this risk per unit of exposure.

Fractionating the dose is an important practical concept: a total dose that is lethal if received all at once may be non-toxic when spread over time, as the body has time to detoxify and repair. For example, 30 mg of strychnine at once could be fatal, while 3 mg daily for ten days would not.

02 Quantification

Measures of Exposure & Types of Dose

Exposure to poisons can be intentional or unintentional. The key variable determining outcome is the dose — the amount of substance received. Several parameters characterize exposure precisely: the amount administered at one time, the number of doses, the frequency, and the total duration of treatment.

🧮
Types of Dose

Exposure dose — what is encountered in the environment.

Absorbed dose — what actually enters the body from the exposed dose.

Administered dose — quantity given orally or by injection.

Total dose — sum of all individual doses over the treatment period.

📏
Units of Dose

The standard unit is the gram (g). Common expressions include mg (10⁻³g), µg (10⁻⁶g), ng (10⁻⁹g), and pg (10⁻¹²g). In toxicology, dose is usually expressed as mg/kg body weight, allowing comparison across species and body sizes. Adding duration gives mg/kg/day for chronic exposures.

⚠️ Environmental Exposure Units

Contaminant levels in the environment are expressed as concentration per medium: mg/L for liquids, mg/g for solids, mg/m³ for air. Parts per million (ppm) = mg/kg; parts per billion (ppb) = µg/kg. One teaspoon per 1,000 gallons of water equals approximately 1 ppm.

03 Dose Estimates

Response Types & Dose Estimates of Toxicity

Toxic effects represent changes from the normal state and can occur at the molecular, cellular, organ, or whole-organism level. They may be local or systemic, reversible or irreversible, immediate or delayed. Within any population, most individuals will show a similar response (the majority), but sensitive individuals respond at lower doses and resistant individuals at higher doses.

📉
LD₅₀ — Lethal Dose 50%

The most widely used acute toxicity measure. It is the statistically derived dose at which 50% of a test population is expected to die. Lower LD₅₀ = more potent/dangerous. Allows comparison across chemicals and species. Dioxin (TCDD) has an LD₅₀ of just 0.001 mg/kg in some species, while table salt is 4,000 mg/kg.

🎯
NOAEL & LOAEL

NOAELNo Observed Adverse Effect Level: the highest dose at which no harmful effects are seen in studies. — No Observed Adverse Effect Level: the highest dose showing no toxic effects.

LOAEL — Lowest Observed Adverse Effect Level: the lowest dose at which an adverse effect is first detected.

💊
Effective Dose (ED)

Indicates beneficial effect in a population. ED₅₀ = effective for 50% of the population. Used to quantify the therapeutic action of drugs (e.g. pain relief, sedation).

⚡
Therapeutic Index (TI)

TI = LD₅₀ ÷ ED₅₀. Measures relative safety of a drug. A higher TI = safer drug (wide margin between effective and lethal dose). A TI of 10 is much safer than a TI of 3.

🛡️
Margin of Safety (MOS)

MOS = LD₀₁ ÷ ED₉₉. Compares the lethal dose for the most sensitive individual to the effective dose for the most resistant. A MOS <1 demands extreme clinical caution.

📌 Toxicity Scale (Pesticide Labeling)

Category I (Highly toxic, LD₅₀ <50 mg/kg): labelled DANGER-POISON with skull and crossbones. Category II (Moderately toxic, 51–500 mg/kg): WARNING. Category III (Slightly toxic, >500 mg/kg): CAUTION. Category IV (>2,000 mg/kg): no special label required.

04 ADME

Toxicokinetics vs. Toxicodynamics

ToxicokineticsToxicokinetics: quantitative description of how a substance moves through the body over time — absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). is the characterization of how a substance gets into the body and what happens to it — the time course of ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion). It answers: what does the body do to the toxicant?

Toxicodynamics, by contrast, describes what the toxicant does to the body — the processes and changes at target tissues including receptor binding and metabolic effects that result in the adverse effect.

🫁
A
Absorption
Xenobiotic enters systemic circulation. First rate-limiting step. No absorption = no toxicity.
🩸
D
Distribution
Transported from blood to target and storage sites throughout the body.
⚗️
M
Metabolism
Biotransformed (mainly in liver) into more polar, excretable metabolites.
🚽
E
Excretion
Eliminated from the body via urine, feces, breath, or other routes.
🔑 How Toxicokinetics Influences Toxicity

A poorly absorbed xenobiotic may cause no toxicity despite high inherent potency. Distribution to tissues other than the target organ dilutes effective concentration. Two chemicals with equal absorption may differ in toxicity based on their biotransformation pathways. Rapid elimination reduces toxicity; slow elimination allows accumulation. When the rate of absorption exceeds the rate of elimination, the xenobiotic accumulates to toxic concentrations at the target site.

05 Absorption

Absorption — Entry into the Body

Absorption is the transfer of a xenobiotic from the site of exposure into the systemic circulation. It is the first and rate-limiting step in toxicokinetics. Without absorption, no systemic toxicity occurs.

👄
Oral / Gastrointestinal
The primary route for food/water contaminants and drugs. Absorption occurs throughout the GI tract. Stomach (pH 1–3) favours weak acids; small intestine (pH 7–8, surface area ~200 m²) is the major absorption site for most xenobiotics including alkaline compounds. Colon absorption is minimal.
💨
Inhalation / Lung
Gases, vapours, and aerosols are absorbed here. Solubility in blood determines gas absorption rate. Very fine particles (<1 µm diameter) reach the alveoli and enter the bloodstream directly. Particle size determines how deep into the respiratory tract a substance penetrates.
🖐️
Dermal / Skin
Human skin is a multilayered barrier; the rate-limiting layer is the stratum corneum (outermost epidermis). Important for pesticides and occupational chemicals. Lipid solubility, skin hydration, and the body region (e.g. scrotal skin absorbs far more than the sole of the foot) are critical factors.

Factors Affecting Absorption

Chemical Factors

Molecular weight: smaller molecules are more readily absorbed.

Lipid solubilityLipid solubility: measured by the lipid/water partition coefficient (log P). Positive log P = lipid-soluble (better absorbed through membranes); negative log P = water-soluble.: higher lipid solubility = greater membrane penetration.

Ionization (pKa): the ionized form has low lipid solubility and crosses membranes poorly. Weak acids are best absorbed in the acidic stomach; weak bases in the alkaline intestine.

Concentration at site & physical form: higher concentration increases absorption rate; solid chemicals must dissolve first and are absorbed more slowly.

Site Factors & Ion Trap

Blood flow: high perfusion at the absorption site increases absorption rate significantly.

Surface area: larger surface area enables proportionally more absorption — this explains why the small intestine (200 m²) is the dominant absorption organ.

Ion trap phenomenon: pH manipulation can trap ionized xenobiotics in a compartment, accelerating their excretion. Weak acids are trapped and excreted faster in alkaline urine; weak bases in acidic urine. This is exploited clinically in acute poisonings to hasten elimination.

Henderson-Hasselbalch for acids: pKa – pH = log [nonionized] / [ionized]
Henderson-Hasselbalch for bases: pKa – pH = log [ionized] / [nonionized]
06 Membranes

Transmembrane Transport Mechanisms

To reach systemic circulation, xenobiotics must cross cell membranes — phospholipid bilayers with embedded proteins that serve as channels, receptors, and transporters. The basic membrane structure consists of a polar (hydrophilic) head region facing the exterior and a non-polar (lipophilic) tail region in the interior.

01
Passive Diffusion (Simple Diffusion)

The most common mechanism. Requires no energy or carrier proteins. Driven entirely by the concentration gradient (high → low). Lipid-soluble chemicals diffuse through the lipid domain; small hydrophilic molecules (MW <600) can cross through aqueous pores.

02
Facilitated Diffusion

Uses a protein carrier but requires no energy and does not transport against a concentration gradient. Solute carriers (SLCs) — including organic anion transporters (OAT) and organic cation transporters (OCT) — are important in renal and hepatic handling of xenobiotics.

03
Active Transport

Transports xenobiotics against a concentration gradient using specific carrier proteins and requiring ATP energy. The ATP-binding cassette (ABC) family includes P-glycoprotein (P-gp), an efflux transporter that pumps chemicals out of cells — clinically important because it contributes to chemotherapy drug resistance in cancer cells. Multidrug resistance protein (MRP) handles conjugated xenobiotic metabolites.

04
Endocytosis (Phagocytosis & Pinocytosis)

For large molecules and particles that cannot cross membranes otherwise. In phagocytosis ("cell eating"), the cell membrane engulfs a solid particle and pinches off a vesicle inside the cell. In pinocytosis ("cell drinking"), liquid droplets or very small particles are taken in by the same mechanism.

07 Distribution

Distribution, Storage & Physiological Barriers

After entering the bloodstream, xenobiotics distribute throughout the body. Only the free (unbound) fraction of the xenobiotic is pharmacologically/toxicologically active. Distribution is a rapid process relative to absorption and elimination, and its extent depends on blood flow, molecular size, lipid solubility, plasma protein binding, and tissue binding.

🩸
Plasma Protein Binding

Xenobiotics bind reversibly to plasma proteins such as albumin and globulins. This binding prolongs half-life in the body, reduces the free active fraction, and affects the dose threshold for toxicity. When a second drug with higher albumin affinity displaces the first, the free (active/toxic) concentration of the displaced drug can increase 100-fold — a critical drug interaction mechanism.

📦
Storage Depots

Fat: lipid-soluble chemicals (DDT, PCBs) accumulate in adipose tissue. Low blood flow makes fat a stable, slowly-releasing depot.

Liver & Kidney: high capacity organs that bind many xenobiotics (metallothionein binds cadmium).

Bone: Lead (Pb) stores in bone (toxic to brain, not bone); fluoride causes skeletal fluorosis; radioactive strontium causes osteosarcoma.

Volume of Distribution: Vd (L) = Dose (mg) ÷ Plasma Concentration (mg/L)
Larger Vd → more extensive distribution to fat, muscle, and tissues
🧠
Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)

Less permeable than most body areas. Formed by capillary endothelial cellsEndothelial cells: thin cells lining blood vessels. In the brain, they form extremely tight junctions that restrict most substances from entering. with tight junctions supported by astrocytes. Only lipid-soluble xenobiotics cross this barrier. Importantly, the BBB is not fully developed at birth, meaning newborns are more vulnerable to neurotoxic substances than adults.

🤰
Placental Barrier

Consists of ~6 cell layers between maternal and fetal circulatory vessels. Once believed to be an absolute barrier until the thalidomide disaster of 1960 proved otherwise. Xenobiotics with MW <1000 can cross (e.g. ethanol, lead). Lipid solubility and plasma protein binding are the key determinants of fetal transfer.

🔄 Redistribution & Bioavailability

Redistribution is the movement of a xenobiotic from its initial distribution site to higher-affinity sites over time. For example, lead initially goes to the liver, then redistributes to bone over weeks. Bioavailability (F) is the fraction of the administered dose reaching systemic circulation. It is 100% for IV administration and less for all other routes. A high first-pass metabolism in the liver significantly reduces oral bioavailability.

08 Excretion

Biotransformation & Excretion Routes

Biotransformation (metabolism) converts xenobiotics into more polar (water-soluble) forms that can be excreted. It occurs primarily in the liver in two phases: Phase I (functionalization — oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) and Phase II (conjugation reactions that attach polar groups). Metabolites may retain biological activity or even be more toxic than the parent compound (bioactivationBioactivation: metabolic conversion of a relatively inactive substance into a more toxic metabolite — e.g. paracetamol → NAPQI, which is hepatotoxic in overdose.).

🫘
Urinary / Renal Excretion
The primary excretion route. Three processes: (1) Glomerular filtration — molecules >60 kDa and protein-bound xenobiotics are excluded; ~99% of filtrate is reabsorbed, 1% excreted as urine. (2) Active tubular secretion at the proximal tubule — handles protein-bound compounds and polar conjugates. (3) Tubular reabsorption at the distal tubule — passive diffusion back into blood. Urine pH is clinically manipulated to trap ionized xenobiotics and accelerate their excretion in poisonings.
💩
Fecal / Biliary Excretion
Via two pathways: (1) Biliary excretion — liver metabolites/conjugates are secreted into bile then the intestine. May exit with feces or re-enter circulation via enterohepatic circulationEnterohepatic circulation: recycling loop where biliary-excreted compounds are reabsorbed from the gut back into the portal blood, returning to the liver. Prolongs drug half-life and can enhance toxicity. — where gut bacteria cleave conjugates, making metabolites lipophilic again for reabsorption, prolonging half-life and enhancing toxicity. Metals (As, Pb, Hg) use this route. (2) Direct intestinal excretion — a slower route for compounds with low biotransformation rates.
🌬️
Respiratory / Exhalation
Important for volatile substances and their gaseous metabolites. The rate of elimination is inversely proportional to the rate of absorption. Occurs by passive diffusion across alveolar membranes. Examples: ethanol (hence breathalyser tests), anaesthetic gases.
🤱
Other Routes (Milk, Sweat, Saliva)
Breast milk (slightly more acidic than plasma at pH ~6.5) is critically important because lipid-soluble xenobiotics diffuse into the mammary gland and are passed to nursing infants — a major concern for environmental toxicant exposure. Sweat can be a significant excretion route under heavy perspiration; toxic substances in sweat may cause dermatitis.
📌 Key Principle — When Rate of Absorption Exceeds Elimination

When a xenobiotic is absorbed faster than it is eliminated, it accumulates in the body, reaching critical concentration at the target site and producing toxicity. This is why the rates of ADME processes together determine whether and how severely a xenobiotic causes harm — not just its inherent chemical potency.
