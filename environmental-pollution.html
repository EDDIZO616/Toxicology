What is the Environment?

The word environment comes from the French word "environ" meaning "to surround." It refers to everything that surrounds living organisms — both abiotic (non-living: air, water, soil) and biotic (living) components. The four basic components are the atmosphere (air), hydrosphere (water), lithosphere (rock/soil), and the biosphere (all living things).

Environmental Chemistry is the scientific study of chemical and biochemical phenomena in natural places — it covers the sources, reactions, transport, effects, and fates of chemical species in air, soil, and water, and how human activity alters these.

Environmental Pollution is any undesirable change in the physical, chemical, or biological characteristics of air, water, or soil that causes harmful effects on living organisms or property. A pollutant is any substance introduced into the environment that causes such adverse effects — it may cause long or short-term damage by altering the growth rate of plant or animal species.

💨 Air Pollution
Definition Air pollution is the excessive concentration of foreign matter in air which adversely affects the well-being of individuals or causes damage to property.

Types of Air Pollutants

Primary Pollutants are emitted directly from a source. Examples: Carbon monoxide (CO), Nitrogen oxides (NOx), Sulphur oxides (SOx), most hydrocarbons, suspended particulate matter (SPM).

Secondary Pollutants form when primary pollutants react with each other or with the atmosphere. Examples: Ozone (O₃), Photochemical smog, Peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), H₂SO₄, HNO₃.

Sources / Causes of Air Pollution

Natural Sources:

  • Natural disasters — volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, cyclones
  • Forest fires — release large amounts of smoke and carbon particles
  • Biological decay of organic matter
  • Pollen grains — trigger allergic reactions
  • Radioactive material — Radon gas from soil and rock

Man-made (Anthropogenic) Sources:

  • Industrial waste — factories release SO₂, CO, particulates
  • Automobiles — exhaust fumes (CO, NOx, hydrocarbons)
  • Thermal power stations — burn fossil fuels, emit SO₂ and particulate matter
  • Nuclear explosions — release radioactive particles
  • Agricultural activities — pesticide spraying, burning of crop residues
  • Disposal of garbage — open burning of solid waste
🏠 Indoor Air Pollution
Definition Indoor air pollution refers to the presence of harmful substances or contaminants inside buildings or enclosed spaces. It describes contaminants in the air inside a building from sources such as cigarette smoking, fuel combustion for heating or cooking, carpets, wallboards, insulation, or radon in the soil beneath the structure.

Urban Indoor Sources

  • Pesticides and household care products
  • Perfumes, hairsprays, furniture polish, glues
  • Air fresheners and wood preservatives
  • Reduced ventilation in modern buildings
  • Tobacco smoke — harms both active and passive smokers

Biological Indoor Pollutants

  • Pollen from plants
  • Dust mites, pet hair and dander
  • Fungi and parasites
  • Mold and bacteria
  • Formaldehyde from carpets and particle boards — causes allergies
  • Asbestos — linked to lung cancer

Sources of Radon Indoors

Natural: Earth and rock beneath the home, well water, outdoor air seeping in.

Artificial: Building materials like gypsum board, concrete blocks, wood panelling, insulation; daily-life materials like low-density plastics and paints.

Solutions to Indoor Air Pollution

Prevention:

  • Cover ceiling tiles and AC duct linings to prevent release of mineral fibres
  • Ban or restrict smoking to well-ventilated areas
  • Set stricter formaldehyde emission standards for carpets, furniture, building materials
  • Prevent radon infiltration
  • Use less polluting substitutes for harmful cleaning agents and paints

Cleanup / Dilution:

  • Use adjustable fresh air vents in workspaces
  • Increase intake of outside air and change air frequently
  • Circulate building air through rooftop greenhouses
  • Use exhaust hoods for gas stoves and appliances
  • Install efficient chimneys for wood-burning stoves
🌫️ Odour Pollution
Definition Odour pollution refers to the release of offensive, unpleasant, and undesirable odours of objectionable proportions into the environment.

Sources of Odour Pollution

Industrial operations, livestock operations, agricultural activities, vehicular emissions, sewage and waste treatment plants, solid waste landfills, decaying vegetation, poultry farms, tanneries, slaughterhouses, and food/meat processing units.

Key chemical odour sources include chlorine, hydrogen sulphide, organic matter, septic contamination, and methane gas. Treatment methods include activated carbon filtration, air stripping, oxidation, and filtration.

Health Effects of Odour Pollution

  • Respiratory problems — irritation of the airways
  • Nausea — uneasiness of the stomach that often precedes vomiting
  • Insomnia — a sleep disorder characterised by difficulty falling or staying asleep
  • Physical discomfort — general bodily unease
  • Annoyance — an unpleasant mental state characterised by irritation and distraction from conscious thinking
💧 Water Pollution
Definition Water pollution is the introduction of contaminants into water bodies (rivers, lakes, oceans, groundwater) that makes the water harmful to humans, animals, plants, or the overall ecosystem.

Point-Source Pollution

Pollution that comes from a single, identifiable source — such as an oil spill, factory discharge pipe, or sewage outlet. It is easier to regulate because the source is traceable.

Non-Point-Source Pollution

Pollution that comes from many diffuse sources — such as agricultural runoff, road drainage, and urban stormwater. Much harder to control because there is no single discharge point.

Nutrients & Eutrophication

Nutrients (mainly nitrogen and phosphorus) are essential for plant growth. However, when they enter water bodies in excess — through wastewater and fertiliser runoff — they cause over-enrichment, leading to explosive growth of algae and aquatic plants.

This process (eutrophication) depletes dissolved oxygen (DO) in the water as algae decompose, creating dead zones where fish and other aquatic life cannot survive.

Control of Water Pollution (Marine & Freshwater)

  • Municipal and industrial waste must be treated before discharge into water bodies
  • Coastal waste should be periodically analysed to detect pollution levels
  • Soil erosion in coastal land should be arrested using suitable techniques
  • Recreation beaches must be maintained to meet hygienic and aesthetic standards
  • Wastewater treatment plants remove contaminants through physical, chemical, and biological processes before water is released
⚠️ Effects of Pollution on Health, Plants & Materials
Key Fact An average person breathes about 22,000 times a day, taking in approximately 16 kg of air. The health impact depends on the nature and concentration of pollutants, duration of exposure, and age of the person.

Effects on Human Health

Acute (short-term): Eye irritation (caused by NOx, O₃, PAN, smog, particulates), headaches, sore throat, stinging eyes, nausea, skin irritation.

Chronic (long-term): Long-duration exposure leads to lung cancer, heart disease, and damage to the brain, nerves, liver, and kidneys.

High concentrations of SO₂, NO₂, suspended particulate matter (SPM), and photochemical smog aggravate chronic pulmonary diseases such as bronchitis and asthma.

Pollen and various particulates can trigger asthmatic attacks. Carbon monoxide (CO) is particularly dangerous — it binds to haemoglobin with 250 times greater affinity than oxygen, preventing oxygen delivery to tissues.

Effects on Plants

  • Decreased agricultural yield and suppressed growth of vegetables
  • Leaf injury and damage to young plants
  • Decreased growth rate and increased death rate in flora
  • Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) causes leaf tissue to turn white, brown, or yellow — a by-product of burning fossil fuels (oil, coal, gasoline)
  • Ozone (O₃) damage appears as mottled yellow, black, or brown spots on leaves; eventually causes leaf drop
  • Fluorides and peroxyacyl nitrate (PAN) also damage leaf tissue and can kill entire plants

Effects on Materials & Structures

  • SO₂, in the presence of oxygen and moisture, converts to H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid) — corroding metals
  • H₂SO₄ reacts with limestone, marble, and other building materials, causing deterioration (acid rain effect)
  • SO₂, O₃, H₂S, and aerosols damage protective coatings and paints on surfaces
  • O₃ and PAN cause cracking of rubber and electrical insulations
  • Suspended Particulate Matter (SPM) soils and erodes building facades and damages artwork
🛡️ Air Pollution Control
Key Principle Air pollution cannot be fully prevented, but it can be effectively controlled through two broad strategies: preventative (source control) measures, and engineering control using specialised equipment.

Preventative Measures (Source Control)

  • Selection of suitable, cleaner fuels — low sulphur coal in power plants, use of Compressed Natural Gas (CNG)
  • Modification of industrial processes to reduce emissions
  • Careful selection of site and zoning for industrial units (away from residential areas)
  • Removal of leaded petrol — lead is a major air pollutant that causes neurological damage
  • Implementation of industrial acts to control harmful gas emissions
  • Natural disaster management teams minimise the effects of forest fires and volcanic eruptions

Control Equipment

1. Gravitational Settling Chamber — used to remove larger particles (size >50 µm). Flue gas velocity is reduced in a large chamber so particles settle under gravitational force. Advantages: low cost, easy design, low maintenance. Disadvantages: large space needed, cannot collect fine particles.

2. Scrubbers — particulate matter is incorporated into liquid droplets and removed from the gas stream. Types include spray tower, Venturi scrubber, and cyclone scrubber. Flue gas is pushed upward against a downward-falling water current — particles mix with water and fall out. Advantages: simultaneously removes particulates and gaseous pollutants, can cool hot gases. Disadvantages: large amounts of wastewater produced, freezing problems in cold climates.
