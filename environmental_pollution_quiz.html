B Section B — Short Answer Questions (5 × 5 marks)
5 Marks
Short Answer Question 1
Distinguish between primary and secondary air pollutants. Give two examples of each and explain how secondary pollutants are formed. (5 marks)
Model Answer Primary pollutants are emitted directly from a source into the atmosphere without any transformation. Examples include carbon monoxide (CO) emitted from vehicle exhaust, and sulphur dioxide (SO₂) released from burning coal in power stations. (2 marks)

Secondary pollutants are not emitted directly but are formed when primary pollutants react with each other or with other atmospheric components (sunlight, moisture, oxygen). Examples include ozone (O₃) and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), both formed in photochemical smog reactions. (2 marks)

Formation of secondary pollutants involves photochemical reactions: for example, NO₂ absorbs ultraviolet sunlight and splits into NO and a reactive oxygen atom, which combines with O₂ to form O₃. PAN forms when reactive hydrocarbons react with NOₓ in sunlight. (1 mark)
5 Marks
Short Answer Question 2
List and explain FIVE health effects that can result from exposure to indoor air pollutants. (5 marks)
Model Answer Any five of the following, 1 mark each:

1. Lung cancer — from exposure to asbestos fibres, radon-222 gas, tobacco smoke, and benzo-a-pyrene from woodstoves.
2. Cardiovascular / heart disease — carbon monoxide from faulty furnaces displaces oxygen in the blood, stressing the heart; tobacco smoke also contributes.
3. Nerve disorders — tetrachloroethylene (dry-cleaning fluid) and styrene cause damage to the central nervous system.
4. Liver and kidney damage — styrene (carpets, plastics) and tetrachloroethylene damage these organs with chronic exposure.
5. Allergic reactions, sneezing, and breathing difficulty — biological pollutants such as dust mites, fungal spores, pet dander, and formaldehyde from carpets trigger these responses.
6. Carbon monoxide poisoning (headache and death) — CO binds haemoglobin 250× more strongly than O₂, preventing oxygen delivery to tissues.
7. Asbestosis — scarring of lung tissue due to prolonged asbestos inhalation.
5 Marks
Short Answer Question 3
Explain the process of eutrophication in water bodies. Include the causes, the sequence of events, and the ultimate ecological impact. (5 marks)
Model Answer Cause (1 mark): Eutrophication is triggered by excessive input of plant nutrients — mainly nitrogen and phosphorus — into water bodies through agricultural fertiliser runoff and discharge of untreated wastewater/sewage.

Sequence of events (3 marks):
Step 1 — Over-enrichment of the water with nutrients stimulates explosive and uncontrolled growth of algae and aquatic plants (algal bloom).
Step 2 — As the dense algal layer blocks sunlight, submerged plants die. The dead algae and plants are decomposed by bacteria, a process that consumes large amounts of dissolved oxygen (DO).
Step 3 — Dissolved oxygen levels drop dramatically, creating hypoxic or anoxic (oxygen-depleted) zones known as dead zones.

Ecological impact (1 mark): Fish, invertebrates, and other aquatic organisms cannot survive in dead zones and die en masse, resulting in a catastrophic loss of aquatic biodiversity and collapse of the local ecosystem.
5 Marks
Short Answer Question 4
Describe the effects of air pollution on materials and structures. Mention at least four specific pollutants and the damage each causes. (5 marks)
Model Answer 1. SO₂ — in the presence of oxygen and moisture, converts to sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄). This acid corrodes metals such as iron and steel, and reacts with limestone, marble, and concrete, causing significant deterioration of buildings and monuments (acid rain effect). (1.5 marks)

2. Ozone (O₃) and PAN — these powerful oxidants cause cracking and brittleness in rubber products and electrical insulation materials, compromising their function. They also damage protective surface coatings and paints. (1.5 marks)

3. H₂S and aerosols — hydrogen sulphide tarnishes metals and discolours paints; aerosols deposit on surfaces and damage protective coatings. (1 mark)

4. Suspended Particulate Matter (SPM) — soils building facades and surfaces, physically erodes stonework over time, and damages artwork and cultural heritage objects. (1 mark)
5 Marks
Short Answer Question 5
Outline FIVE preventative (source control) measures that can be used to reduce outdoor air pollution. (5 marks)
Model Answer 1 mark for each clearly explained measure:

1. Selection of cleaner fuels — switching to low-sulphur coal in power plants and adopting Compressed Natural Gas (CNG) for vehicles significantly reduces SO₂ and NOₓ emissions.
2. Modification of industrial processes — redesigning combustion systems, using catalytic converters, and improving process efficiency to minimise pollutant generation at source.
3. Industrial zoning and site selection — locating factories, power plants, and industrial units away from densely populated residential areas to reduce human exposure.
4. Removal of leaded petrol — phasing out leaded petrol eliminates lead as an airborne pollutant, preventing the neurological damage associated with lead exposure.
5. Legislative and regulatory controls — implementing and enforcing industrial emission standards and environmental acts that set legal limits on the release of harmful gases from factories and vehicles.
C Section C — Case Study Questions (5 × 5 marks)
5 Marks — Case Study
Case Study Question 1
Read the scenario and answer the question that follows.
A factory located near a river discharges its untreated wastewater containing high levels of nitrogen and phosphorus compounds directly into the river. Within a few months, residents downstream notice that the river has turned green and murky, fish are dying, and there is a foul smell. Environmental officers collect water samples and find dissolved oxygen levels near zero.
Question: Using your knowledge of water pollution, explain what process has occurred in this river, the mechanism behind it, and recommend THREE measures the factory should adopt to prevent this situation. (5 marks)
Model Answer Process identified — Eutrophication (1 mark): The discharge of nitrogen and phosphorus-rich wastewater has caused eutrophication of the river.

Mechanism (2 marks): The excess nutrients caused an explosive algal bloom (the green, murky appearance). As algae died and decomposed, bacteria consumed large amounts of dissolved oxygen, driving DO levels to near zero. This created a dead zone, explaining the mass fish death and foul smell from anaerobic decomposition.

Three recommended control measures (2 marks — any two well-explained earn full marks):
1. Install a wastewater treatment plant to remove nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) through biological and chemical treatment before discharge.
2. Reduce use of phosphorus-based chemicals in industrial processes and substitute with less harmful alternatives.
3. Comply with discharge standards set by environmental regulators and regularly analyse discharged water for pollutant levels.
4. Implement constructed wetlands as a natural tertiary treatment system to absorb excess nutrients before water enters the river.
5 Marks — Case Study
Case Study Question 2
Read the scenario and answer the question that follows.
A newly renovated office building in the city is fitted with wall-to-wall carpets, particle board furniture, and an energy-efficient sealed ventilation system. Within weeks of occupation, workers begin reporting persistent headaches, eye irritation, difficulty breathing, and one employee is diagnosed with an allergic respiratory condition. The building manager insists the building is "brand new and clean."
Question: Identify the likely indoor pollutants responsible for these health effects. Explain the sources of each pollutant and suggest THREE practical solutions the building manager should implement. (5 marks)
Model Answer Likely pollutants and sources (2 marks):
1. Formaldehyde — released from new carpets, particle board furniture and adhesives. Causes allergic reactions, eye irritation, and respiratory problems.
2. Styrene — from carpets and plastic components; can cause kidney and liver damage with prolonged exposure.
3. Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) from paints, glues, and wood preservatives used in renovation — cause headaches and breathing difficulty.

Compounding factor (1 mark): The sealed, energy-efficient ventilation system prevents dilution of indoor pollutants with fresh outdoor air, concentrating all contaminants at harmful levels (this phenomenon is called Sick Building Syndrome).

Three practical solutions (2 marks):
1. Increase ventilation — install adjustable fresh air vents and increase the rate of outdoor air intake to dilute and flush out indoor contaminants.
2. Set emission standards — specify low-formaldehyde and low-VOC materials for carpets, furniture, paints, and adhesives in future procurement.
3. Allow the building to "off-gas" — after renovation, ventilate the building intensively for several weeks before occupation to allow volatile chemicals to dissipate.

Case Study Question 3
Read the scenario and answer the question that follows.
A city's traffic authority reports that vehicle numbers have doubled over the past five years. Air quality monitoring stations record increasing levels of carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), and suspended particulate matter (SPM). Hospitals report a rise in emergency admissions for respiratory and cardiovascular complaints, especially during morning and evening rush hours.
Question: Explain the specific health mechanisms by which CO and NOₓ from vehicle emissions harm the human body. Propose FOUR measures the city authority could implement to reduce air pollution from vehicles. (5 marks)
Model Answer Health mechanism of CO (1.5 marks): Carbon monoxide (CO) binds to haemoglobin with 250 times greater affinity than oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin. This prevents haemoglobin from carrying oxygen to body tissues, causing tissue hypoxia (oxygen starvation). Symptoms include headaches, dizziness, irregular heartbeat, and at high concentrations, death. People with existing heart conditions are especially vulnerable because the heart compensates by working harder.

Health mechanism of NOₓ (1.5 marks): Nitrogen oxides (NO₂) irritate the respiratory tract, causing inflammation of the airways. Chronic exposure aggravates bronchitis and asthma. NOₓ also reacts in sunlight to produce secondary pollutants such as ozone (O₃) and PAN, which further irritate the eyes and airways and contribute to photochemical smog.

Four measures for the city authority (2 marks — 0.5 each):
1. Mandate the use of CNG or electric vehicles for public transport and city fleets.
2. Introduce and enforce vehicle emission standards requiring catalytic converters on all petrol and diesel vehicles.
3. Implement congestion pricing or traffic demand management to reduce vehicle numbers during peak hours.
4. Expand urban tree cover and green corridors to absorb CO₂ and particulate matter naturally.
5 Marks — Case Study
Case Study Question 4
Read the scenario and answer the question that follows.
A farmer in a rural area burns crop residues after harvest to clear fields quickly. At the same time, a nearby industrial estate releases SO₂ and particulate matter into the atmosphere. Several kilometres away, a limestone cathedral and metal sculptures in the town square are showing rapid deterioration — the stone is crumbling and the metal is heavily corroded. Local trees show signs of leaf discolouration and reduced fruit yield.
Question: Explain the chemical processes responsible for the damage observed on the cathedral, metal sculptures, and trees. Identify the specific pollutants involved and suggest TWO source-control measures for each of the two pollution sources described. (5 marks)
Model Answer Damage to cathedral — acid rain mechanism (1.5 marks): SO₂ emitted by the industrial estate reacts with atmospheric oxygen and moisture to form sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄): SO₂ + O → SO₃; SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄. Acid rain containing H₂SO₄ reacts with the calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) in limestone, converting it to calcium sulphate which is soluble and washes away, causing the stone to crumble. Metals are directly corroded by the acid.

Damage to trees (1 mark): SO₂ causes leaf tissue to turn white, brown, or yellow (bleaching of chlorophyll), reducing photosynthesis and hence suppressing growth and fruit yield. Particulate matter depositing on leaves also blocks stomata and reduces light absorption.

Source-control for the industrial estate (1.5 marks — any two):
1. Install scrubbers or flue gas desulphurisation (FGD) systems to remove SO₂ before discharge.
2. Switch to low-sulphur fuels or natural gas in industrial furnaces.
3. Install gravitational settling chambers or bag filters to capture particulate matter.

Source-control for agricultural burning (1 mark — any two):
1. Ban open burning of crop residues and provide alternative disposal methods such as composting or incorporation into soil.
2. Provide incentives for farmers to use crop residue as animal feed or bioenergy feedstock rather than burning.

Case Study Question 5
Read the scenario and answer the question that follows.
Residents living near a large poultry farm and a municipal sewage treatment plant have lodged complaints with local authorities. They report persistent foul smells, difficulty sleeping, nausea, and general irritability. A government health inspector visits the site and records high concentrations of hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) and methane. The farm owner argues that the smells are natural and harmless.
Question: Is the farm owner correct? Justify your answer by explaining what odour pollution is, identifying the specific pollutants present, their health effects on the residents, and recommending THREE control measures the authorities could enforce. (5 marks)
Model Answer The farm owner is incorrect (0.5 marks).

Definition and justification (1 mark): Odour pollution is defined as the release of offensive, unpleasant, and undesirable odours of objectionable proportions into the environment. Even if the substances are naturally occurring, once they are released in concentrations that cause harm to surrounding communities, they constitute pollution. Both poultry farms and sewage treatment plants are recognised sources of odour pollution.

Pollutants and health effects (2 marks):
— Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S): produced by the anaerobic decomposition of organic matter. Even at low concentrations it causes respiratory irritation; at higher concentrations it is toxic to the nervous system.
— Methane: produced from decomposing organic waste; contributes to odour and is an asphyxiant at high concentrations.
— Residents' reported symptoms (nausea, insomnia, respiratory problems, and annoyance) are all recognised health effects of odour pollution, confirming the harm.

Three control measures (1.5 marks):
1. Require installation of biofilters or activated carbon filtration systems at the farm and sewage plant to trap and neutralise odorous gases before release.
2. Enforce setback (buffer zone) regulations that prohibit poultry farms and waste facilities from operating within a specified distance of residential areas.
3. Mandate proper covering and sealing of manure storage facilities and sewage digesters to prevent escape of H₂S and methane into the surrounding atmosphere.
