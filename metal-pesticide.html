Arsenic (As)
Metalloid · Atomic No. 33 · MW 74.92 g/mol
🌍 Epidemiological Context

The groundwater contamination of arsenic in Bangladesh represents the largest population poisoning in history, with millions exposed since the 1970s. Arsenic is ubiquitous in the environment and highly toxic in its inorganic form.

⚛️ Toxic Forms
  • Trivalent As(III) — Arsenite: Most toxic — slower excretion rate, principal toxic form. Inhibits sulfhydryl-containing enzymes.
  • Pentavalent As(V) — Arsenate: Less toxic alone, but can be converted to As(III) in vivo, making it indirectly dangerous.
  • Inorganic arsenic (in contaminated water, rice, grains): Most harmful type.
  • Organic arsenic (dimethylarsinic acid): Less toxic; principal detox product after methylation.
⚡ Mechanism of Toxicity
  • Binds sulfhydryl (SH) groups in proteins and enzymes → non-specific cell damage.
  • Inhibits succinic dehydrogenase and uncouples oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria.
  • Chelates alpha-lipoic acid → blocks pyruvate dehydrogenase → disrupts Krebs cycle energy production.
  • Arsenolysis: Arsenic anions substitute for phosphate, disrupting phosphorylation reactions.
  • Capillary poison: increases vascular permeability → edema and hemorrhage.
🔄 Toxicokinetics (ADME)

Absorption

Absorbed via inhalation, GI tract ingestion, and skin. After absorption, rapidly binds the globin portion of haemoglobin → distributes to blood.

Distribution

Within 24 hrs redistributes to liver, kidney, spleen, lung, GI tract. Concentrates in nails, hair, and cancellous bone (replaces phosphorus). Crosses placenta; found in human milk.

Biotransformation

Undergoes methylation in the liver → dimethylarsenic (DMA) as the principal detox product. Methylated forms have a longer half-life. Undergoes enterohepatic circulation.

Elimination

Primarily renal excretion as methylated arsenic. Minor routes: feces, sweat, bile. Safe limit: urinary excretion <100 µg/24 hrs.

🩺 Clinical Presentation — Arsenicosis Stages
Stage/TimeframeKey Features
Acute (≤7 days) Metallic/garlicky taste, xerostomia, dysphagia, profuse vomiting (projectile, dark brown), rice-water stools (later bloody), renal damage (oliguria, proteinuria, haematuria), skeletal muscle cramps, periorbital edema.
Sub-acute (1 wk–1 mo) Peripheral neuropathy, cardiac dysrhythmias, hypotension, pigmentation changes, keratosis, renal insufficiency, haemolysis, confusion.
Chronic Stage 1 GI disturbances: weight loss, anorexia, colicky pain, constipation/diarrhoea.
Chronic Stage 2 — Catarrhal Dry/itchy skin, harsh voice, photophobia, conjunctivitis, hepatomegaly (cirrhotic), chronic nephritis.
Chronic Stage 3 — Skin Raindrop pigmentation (flexors, nipples, abdomen), hyperkeratosis of palms and soles, desquamation.
Chronic Stage 4 — Neurological Peripheral neuritis — "glove and stocking" anaesthesia, encephalopathy in some cases.
Cancers (long-term) Skin, lung, bladder, kidney, liver cancers — especially from drinking water exposure over 20–40 years. Blackfoot disease (Taiwan). Known carcinogen.
🔬 Diagnosis
  • Recent: Urine — arsenic ions present shortly after ingestion. Urinary As >100 µg/24 hrs → toxic exposure. Proteinuria, casts, albuminuria present.
  • Acute (blood): Blood As 0.9 µg/dl; monocytic hypochromic anaemia, leukocytosis, mild eosinophilia.
  • Chronic: Hair/nails — >300 ppm or >100 mg As/100 g specimen → diagnostic.
  • Liver: ↑ alkaline phosphatase, ↑ bilirubin, urobilinogen in urine.
💊 Treatment
  • Supportive care — hydration, electrolyte replacement.
  • GI decontamination: Gastric lavage (1% sodium thiosulfate), whole bowel irrigation, demulcents (ghee, barley water), purgatives (castor oil, MgSO₄).
  • Chelation therapy:
    • BAL (dimercaprol) 3–5 mg/kg every 4 hrs
    • DMSA 10 mg/kg PO for 5 days
    • Penicillamine 100 mg/kg in 4 divided doses for 4–8 days after initial BAL
  • Morphine for pain; hemodialysis for renal failure.

Mercury (Hg)
Hydrargyria / Mercurialism · Heavy Metal
⚠️ Key Principle: Form Determines Toxicity

Mercury exists in three distinct chemical forms, each with different routes of entry, distribution, and toxicological profile.

Hg⁰ Elemental
  • Most volatile form; liquid or vapour at room temperature.
  • Absorbed mainly via lungs (80%); GI absorption negligible (0.01%).
  • Crosses blood-brain barrier (BBB) and placenta.
  • Common source: dental amalgam (most common form of clinical exposure), thermometers, barometers.
Hg⁺ Inorganic
  • Main entry: lungs (80%).
  • Cannot cross BBB or placental barrier → accumulates in kidneys.
  • Forms glutathione/cysteine complexes in liver → excreted in bile.
  • Historical uses: laxatives, skin-lightening creams, latex paint.
  • Found in neonatal brains (absent fully formed BBB).
CH₃Hg Organic
  • Mainly methylmercury — highly toxic, lipophilic.
  • Absorbed excellently via GI tract; crosses BBB and placenta.
  • Accumulates in brain and kidney.
  • Source: contaminated fish, fungicides, pesticides.
  • Cooking does not reduce methylmercury in fish.
⚡ Mechanism of Toxicity
  • High-affinity binding to sulfhydryl (SH) groups of cellular proteins → non-specific cell injury / cell death.
  • Disrupts microtubule formation.
  • Inhibits key enzymes; induces oxidative stress.
  • Disrupts protein and DNA synthesis.
  • Triggers autoimmune responses.
  • Overexpresses metallothionein and glutathione-related genes in tissues.
⚡ Acute Poisoning

Inhalation of vapour

  • Chemical pneumonia, pulmonary oedema
  • Gingivostomatitis, hypersalivation
  • CNS: ataxia, restricted visual field, paresis, delirium, polyneuropathy

Ingestion of Hg salts

  • Acrid metallic taste, corrosion of mouth and tongue (grey-white coating)
  • Burning pain in mouth, stomach, abdomen
  • Blood-stained stools, suppressed urine with albumin and blood
  • Rapid, irregular pulse → circulatory failure
  • After 1–3 days: renal tubular necrosis → polyuria, albuminuria, uraemia, acidosis
  • Thrombocytopenia, bone marrow depression
🕰️ Chronic Poisoning (Hydrargyria)
  • Symptomatic at blood Hg >100 mg/mL or urinary excretion >300 mg/mL/day.
  • Excessive salivation, swollen salivary glands, foul breath, inflamed/ulcerated gums, brownish-blue gum line, tooth loosening.
  • Mercura lentis: Brownish anterior lens capsule reflex (bilateral; no effect on visual acuity) — pathognomonic sign.
  • Acrodynia (Pink Disease): Generalised erythematous, eczematous rash, thickened skin on hands and feet.
  • Mercurial tremors (Danbury Tremors / Hatter's Shake): Coarse tremors starting in hands → lips, tongue, arms, legs. Excited by voluntary movement, absent during sleep. Must be differentiated from thyrotoxicosis.
  • Erethism (Mad Hatter Syndrome): Shyness, timidity, irritability, loss of memory, insomnia, hallucinations, suicidal melancholia.
📌 Minamata Disease — Classic Case Study

Chronic methylmercury poisoning from contaminated fish consumption (Minamata Bay, Japan, 1932–1968). Features: disturbance in hand coordination, gait, speech, chewing/swallowing difficulty, tremors, rigidity, seizures, and clouding of consciousness. Over 1,000 deaths; thousands permanently disabled.

🔬 Diagnosis
  • Fatal dose (FD): 1–4 g mercuric chloride; 10–60 mg/kg methylmercury; 10 mg/m³ vapour.
  • Blood Hg: >3.6 µg/dL (toxic).
  • 24-hr urinary Hg: >15 µg/dL.
  • Measured by atomic absorption spectrophotometry.
💊 Treatment
  • Remove from exposure source.
  • Gastric lavage: 250 mL sodium formaldehyde sulphoxylate (reduces Hg perchloride to metallic Hg).
  • Milk, eggs (albumin) to precipitate Hg.
  • BAL (dimercaprol): 3–5 mg/kg 4-hrly for 2 days → 2.5 mg/kg 6-hrly for 1 day → taper.
  • 5–10% glucose in normal saline for diuresis.
  • Periodic medical checkup for occupationally exposed workers.

Lead (Pb)
Plumbism · The Most Toxic Metal · No Safe Level
⚠️ Key Principle: Zero Safe Level

Lead has no biological role in the human body. Any measurable amount may have negative health effects. It is not biodegradable and cannot be metabolized — only redistributed. The most susceptible populations are children, infants, and neonates.

🌐 Routes & Sources of Exposure

Ingestion (Primary)

  • Lead paint — major source in children (peeling/chipping paint).
  • Contaminated food, water, alcohol.
  • Children absorb 50% of ingested lead after meals, up to 100% on empty stomachs vs 20% in adults.

Inhalation (Secondary)

  • Almost all inhaled lead is absorbed.
  • Leaded gasoline emissions (historical + ongoing in some countries).
  • Occupational: smelting, soldering, home renovation, stained glass making.
  • Tobacco and secondhand smoke.

Dermal (Occupational)

  • Organic lead (tetraethyl lead) absorbed through skin — more than inorganic forms.

Endogenous / Transplacental

  • Lead stored in bone is continuously recycled.
  • Crosses placenta — matches maternal blood levels in fetus.
  • Half-life of lead in blood: 20 years.
🔄 Toxicokinetics

Distribution

  • Adults: 95%+ of total body lead stored in bone.
  • Children: ~70% stored in bone — but bone is actively remodelling → continuous release.

Metabolism

  • Inorganic lead: not metabolized in the liver.
  • Organic lead: metabolized in the liver.
  • Cannot be destroyed or changed within the body.

Excretion

  • Primary: urine (glomerular filtration with partial tubular resorption).
  • Secondary: feces via biliary tract (~⅓ of total excreted).
🧠 Organ System Effects
SystemKey Toxic Effects
Nervous System Lead mimics calcium (both are divalent cations) → inappropriate neurotransmitter release, inhibits evoked release. Causes cognition problems, poor executive function, abnormal social behaviour, fine motor difficulty. Children: reduced IQ, learning failure, behaviour problems, developmental delays.
Haematopoietic Inhibits 3 enzymes in haem biosynthesis — most profound effect on δ-ALAD. Results in microcytic anaemia. Basophilic stippling of RBCs is a classic finding.
Renal System Proximal tubular nephropathy, Fanconi-like syndrome, chronic lead nephropathy (tubulointerstitial nephritis), glomerular sclerosis, interstitial fibrosis. Often associated with hypertension and gout (lead-induced hyperuricaemia).
Cardiovascular Hypertension (↑ vascular reactivity), ECG changes, atherosclerosis, ischaemic coronary heart disease, myocarditis, cerebrovascular accidents.
Reproductive Reduced sperm count, increased abnormal sperm. Adverse pregnancy outcomes at high exposure — miscarriages, preterm birth. Transplacental transfer causes serious fetal nervous system damage.
Carcinogenesis Classified as Group 2B carcinogen in animals (insufficient human data). Possible role in renal cancer.
💊 Treatment
  • Primary: Remove from exposure source — critical first step.
  • Chelation therapy warranted when BLL >60 µg/dL in workers.
  • Severe symptoms (usually BLL >100 µg/dL): Chelation reduces lead in blood and soft tissues.
  • EDTA (oral or IV) — standard chelator.
  • DMSA (Dimercaptosuccinic acid) — advantages over EDTA; effective in temporarily reducing BLL. Note: does not improve long-term BLL in children.
🛡️ Prevention
  • Regular wet-dusting of surfaces; wash fruits and vegetables.
  • Ensure adequate dietary calcium and iron (reduces lead absorption).
  • Never give children access to peeling lead paint or old painted toys.
  • Frequent handwashing especially before meals.
  • Avoid high-fat diets (increase lead absorption).
  • Regular meals — more lead absorbed on empty stomach.
☠️
Thallium (Tl)
"The Poisoner's Poison" · "Inheritance Powder" · MW 204.38 g/mol
🔑 Key Facts

Thallium sulphate is the most toxic, freely water-soluble salt. It is odourless, tasteless, and freely soluble — making it historically ideal for homicidal poisoning. Thallium-201 is used clinically in myocardial perfusion imaging.

⚡ Mechanism of Toxicity
  • Tl⁺ mimics K⁺ ions (similar ionic radius) → competes with potassium in vital cellular processes.
  • Substitutes for K⁺ in Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase with 10x greater affinity than potassium → inhibition of the pump → membrane depolarization.
  • Inactivates sulphydryl (SH) groups → increases mitochondrial permeability → water influx and swelling.
  • Inhibits protein synthesis, particularly incorporation of cysteine → prevents keratinization → alopecia.
  • Interacts with riboflavin (Vitamin B2) → forms insoluble compound → disrupts energy production (similar to riboflavin deficiency: peripheral neuropathy + hair loss).
🩺 Clinical Features

Acute Poisoning

  • Onset insidious — maximum effects at 2nd–3rd week.
  • Initial: GI disturbances, hyperaesthesia of soles of feet, polyneuritis, tachycardia.
  • Alopecia (hair loss) — hallmark sign, appears ~15 days after exposure.
  • Nail changes (Mees lines), skin atrophy.
  • Psychotic behaviour, hallucinations, dementia.

Chronic Poisoning

  • Polyneuritic symptoms, sleep disorders, headache, fatigue.
  • Pain especially in joints (ankles, knees, thoracic spine).
  • Hair loss, skin abnormalities, GI dysfunction.
💊 Management of Thallium Poisoning

Decontamination

  • Induce emesis → gastric aspiration → lavage.
  • Activated charcoal (if Prussian Blue unavailable).

Enhanced Elimination

  • Forced diuresis — important; combined with charcoal perfusion → half-life ~1.4 days.
  • Charcoal haemoperfusion — effective within 48 hrs of ingestion.

Antidote — Prussian Blue

  • Prussian Blue (potassium ferric hexacyanoferrate): 10 g dissolved in 100 mL 1.5% mannitol, twice daily — given orally or intraduodenally.
  • Traps Tl⁺ in the gut by exchanging it for K⁺ in the molecule's lattice → insoluble complex, not absorbed.
  • Continue until urinary Tl excretion <0.6 mg/24 hrs.

Note: BAL, EDTA, and penicillamine do NOT accelerate Tl excretion. KCl supplementation may help excretion.

🔬 Diagnosis of Thallium Poisoning
  • Opacities in GI tract / liver on imaging.
  • Decreased colour sensitivity, optic neuropathy.
  • ECG changes.
  • Decreased nerve conduction velocity.
  • Hair microscopy: dark pigment at roots — highly characteristic.
  • Urine Tl elevated (may persist weeks to months).

Pesticide Toxicology
Biocides · Over 1,000 Compounds Worldwide
📌 Why Study Pesticides?

Pesticides are toxic by design — they target biological systems in pests that are often identical or similar to those in humans. Despite developing countries using only 25% of global pesticide production, they account for 99% of pesticide-related deaths due to unsafe use and weaker regulatory systems.

☣️ Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs)

Nine of the 12 POPs listed under the Stockholm Convention (2004) are pesticides.

  • Low water solubility, lipophilic
  • Resist environmental breakdown
  • Accumulate in adipose tissue and food chain
  • Travel long distances; highest levels in marine mammals
  • Concern for endocrine and developmental effects
  • Arctic persistence: 3–8× longer than in temperate climates

BANNED POP Pesticides:

Aldrin Dieldrin Chlordane DDT* Endrin Heptachlor Mirex Toxaphene HCB

*DDT still used for malaria control in some developing countries.

🌫️
Pesticide Exposure & Fate
Routes, Settings, and Environmental Distribution
🚪 Routes of Exposure
  • Ingestion: Breastfeeding, accidental ingestion, residues in food, mouthing behaviour (children).
  • Inhalation: Indoor/outdoor spraying, occupational exposure.
  • Dermal absorption: Accidental contact, occupational, contaminated clothing, medical use (scabies, head lice treatment).
  • Transplacental: Detected in amniotic fluid and fetal tissues from early pregnancy. Meconium OP metabolites are a validated biomarker of prenatal exposure (accumulates from week 16).
📍 Key Exposure Settings
  • Agricultural use — spraying fields, seed treatment, cattle dips.
  • Home use — 74% of US households use ≥1 pesticide; 47% store it in reach of children.
  • Topical pharmaceuticals for head lice / scabies (lindane, malathion).
  • Pet products (flea/tick treatment) — often concentrated as agricultural products.
  • Residues on floors, carpets, furniture, toys, house dust for weeks after application.
  • Playgrounds, lawns, recreational waters.
👶 Why Children Are More Vulnerable
  • Larger skin area relative to body weight → more dermal absorption per kg.
  • Higher respiratory rate → inhale more per kg body weight.
  • Hand-to-mouth behaviour increases ingestion.
  • Immature metabolic pathways (may activate or fail to detoxify pesticides appropriately).
  • Critical windows of development (prenatal, early childhood) — pesticide exposure during brain growth can cause permanent structural/functional changes.
🔬
Pesticide Toxicokinetics
Absorption, Metabolism, Distribution, Elimination
🔄 Metabolic Phase I-II-III

Phase I — Oxidation, Reduction, Hydrolysis. Generally produces more water-soluble, less toxic products.

Phase II — Conjugation with sugars, amino acids, or glutathione. Increases water solubility, reduces toxicity. Facilitates excretion.

Phase III (plants only) — Plants sequester conjugated metabolites into vacuoles or cell walls. Cannot excrete xenobiotics like animals.

⚡ Organophosphate Metabolism

OPs undergo oxidative activation:

  • Oxones (active): The toxic metabolite. E.g., parathion → paraoxon.
  • DAPs — Dialkylphosphates (non-specific): Used as biomarkers of OP exposure for all OP classes.
  • ME — Specific inactive metabolites.

Fat-soluble pesticides accumulate in adipose tissue. Elimination: urine, bile, feces, milk.

⚡
Acute Toxicity & Toxindromes
Mechanisms, Clinical Syndromes, Diagnosis & Treatment
⚡ Key Acute Mechanisms
  • Irritation: Local contact with skin, eyes, mucosa → redness, pain. Most herbicides and fungicides are strong irritants.
  • Allergic sensitization: Common with fungicides.
  • Enzyme inhibition: Cholinesterase inhibited by OPs and carbamates.
  • Oxidative damage: Paraquat promotes superoxide radicals → pulmonary fibrosis.
  • Neurotransmission inhibition: Organochlorines block GABA system, alter calcium homeostasis.
  • Uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation: Glyphosate.

🔍 Diagnosis of Pesticide Exposure

Diagnosis requires high index of suspicion — pesticide poisoning is commonly misdiagnosed as viral illness (e.g., OP poisoning misdiagnosed as infectious diarrhoea). Confirm using: history of exposure + clinical features + laboratory (pesticides/metabolites in blood, urine, breast milk, amniotic fluid, or meconium). Tests available for OPs, organochlorines, carbamates, paraquat, and pyrethroids.

🕰️
Chronic Effects of Pesticides
Neurodevelopment · Cancer · Endocrine Disruption · Immunotoxicity
🧠 Neurodevelopmental Effects
  • Exposure during brain growth causes permanent structural/functional changes: altered synaptogenesis, axonal differentiation, serotonergic system alteration.
  • Chlorpyrifos and diazinon specifically alter axonogenesis and synaptic programming — at doses too low to cause overt cholinesterase inhibition.
  • Chronic OP exposure → memory/concentration problems, unusual fatigue, irritability, depression, delayed polyneuropathy.
  • Acetylcholinesterase has a direct role in axonal outgrowth — OP inhibition may interfere with neurodevelopment, not just cholinergic function.
🧬 Cancer Associations
  • Domestic pesticide exposure associated with:
    • Brain tumours
    • Acute lymphocytic leukaemia (ALL)
    • Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
  • Prenatal permethrin exposure → leukaemia in newborns.
  • Children with certain enzyme polymorphisms (e.g., PON1 genotype) have ↑ risk of ALL with in-utero pesticide exposure (especially 2,4-D).
  • Brain cancer linked to maternal agricultural exposure.
🔁 Endocrine Disruption
  • Pesticides can mimic, block, or trigger inappropriate hormone activity at low doses.
  • Linked to: infertility, low sperm count, early puberty, hormone-dependent cancers (testicular, breast, prostate), altered sex ratio.
  • Can disrupt all steps of hormonal regulation — synthesis, release, transport, receptor binding, post-receptor activation, thyroid function, CNS.
  • Examples: DDT, dieldrin, endosulfan → estrogenic; DDT, vinclozolin → anti-androgenic; ETU, maneb metabolites → anti-thyroid.
🛡️ Immunotoxicity
  • Four potential outcomes to pesticide-immune interaction:
    1. No alteration (most common)
    2. Immune hyperactivation → autoimmune disease
    3. Immunosuppression (decreased neutrophil/macrophage function, ↓ thymocytes, ↓ cytokine secretion)
    4. Hypersensitivity
  • Arctic Inuit infants exposed to p,p'-DDE, HCB, dieldrin → elevated risk of otitis media.
⚠️ Prenatal & Perinatal Concerns
  • Pre-conception parental exposure associated with: fetal death, spontaneous abortion, early childhood cancer.
  • In-utero exposure increases risk of: growth retardation (small for gestational age, low birth weight, reduced length, small head circumference), congenital anomalies (eye, limb, CNS, heart defects).
  • Organochlorine residues detected in breast milk (DDT, HCB, HCH isomers) — breastfed babies are at the top of the food chain.
🛡️
Prevention & Management
Local · National · International
🏠 Local Level
  • Use pesticides only when benefits outweigh risks; try non-chemical pest control first (Integrated Pest Management — IPM).
  • Store only in original containers with child-proof seals, in locked cabinets out of children's reach.
  • Always follow manufacturer safety precautions (gloves, masks, re-entry times).
  • Pregnant women must not apply pesticides. Re-entry times may be considerably longer for pregnant women and infants.
  • Use least hazardous chemical; prefer bait stations over sprays.
🏛️ National Level
  • Education and licensing of pesticide applicators.
  • Bans and restrictions on highly toxic/persistent pesticides (e.g., organochlorine poisoning now rare in countries with domestic use bans).
  • US Food Quality Protection Act (1996): sets residue limits to protect most vulnerable populations (infants, children, fetuses).
  • Voluntary withdrawal of some pesticides (e.g., chlorpyrifos, diazinon) from domestic use.
🌍 International Level
  • Stockholm Convention (2004): controls 9 POP pesticides; signed by 50+ countries.
  • Rotterdam Convention (1998): Prior Informed Consent — countries can refuse hazardous chemicals; covers 22 pesticides.
  • FAO, WHO, ILO promoting safer use and non-chemical alternatives globally.
